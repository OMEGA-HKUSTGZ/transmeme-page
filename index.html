TransMeme

OVERVIEW

Re-creating a meme for another culture, not translating it

A meme works through the interplay of a short text, a visual template and shared cultural knowledge, so restating its caption in another language rarely carries the effect across. TransMeme starts from a task analysis: the target meme has to keep the source's communicative intent, be natural for the target audience, and stay coherent between text and image. It then assigns the resulting challenges to specialised agents that plan the cultural adaptation, rewrite the text, critique the result and revise it before the final image is rendered. On Chinese–English transcreation in both directions, it scores best on all four dimensions in human evaluation and is ranked first most often by an LLM judge on 1,000 memes.

  • 01

    Culture-specific knowledge

    Memes lean on local entities, stereotypes, public events and discourse conventions. The system has to decide whether to keep a reference, reframe it, or replace it with a target-side analogue.

  • 02

    Intent and tone

    Sarcasm, irony, exaggeration and stance are rarely preserved by semantic transfer alone. The text has to rebuild the communicative effect, not only the propositional content.

  • 03

    Multimodal consistency

    Text and image produce the effect together. The system has to judge whether the original image still fits, should be edited, or needs a new realization.

METHOD

Five agents, a shared harness and a conditional visual step

The framework takes a source meme image and produces a target meme. The Interpreter, Coordinator, Adapter, Rewriter and Critic read from and write to a Transcreation Harness that keeps the pipeline's intermediate state: the source understanding, routing decisions, cultural mappings, generated text and critic feedback. Once revision is complete, the Execution Layer applies the approved text with the selected visual strategy, which may preserve the original image, replace the text, or perform stronger visual editing when deeper adaptation is required.

  • Interpreter and Coordinator

    The Interpreter infers the meme's core intent, humor mechanism, emotional stance and background knowledge, and how far the effect depends on the image. The Coordinator sends simple cases down a lightweight path and difficult ones through the full path, and later decides whether to accept a result or return it for rewriting, cultural remapping or a different visual plan.

  • Adapter

    Builds an explicit plan before any text is generated: which culture-specific elements to preserve, reformulate or replace, at one of three adaptation tiers (literal, minimal rephrase, cultural map). It also produces a visual plan stating which visual elements stay fixed and which may be replaced or reinterpreted.

  • Rewriter with DPO

    Generates target-text candidates under the plan's constraints. It is initialized from Qwen3-8B and trained with Direct Preference Optimization and LoRA on human-ranked rewrites of web-sourced colloquial expressions and slang (500 Chinese and 500 English instances), a corpus built independently of the meme benchmark.

  • Critic

    Scores the three candidates the Rewriter produces at inference time, selects the best, and checks intent preservation, cultural adaptation and image-text coherence. When it finds a problem, its feedback names what to revise: the text, the cultural mapping or the visual plan.

RESULTS

Human evaluation, an LLM judge on 1,000 memes, and component analysis

Following the benchmark of Zhao et al. (2026), the evaluation uses 1,000 randomly sampled memes: 500 Chinese-to-English and 500 English-to-Chinese. TransMeme (M) is compared with three baselines under identical inputs and outputs. Five bilingual annotators rate 200 of the memes on four dimensions with a 5-point scale and rank the systems; an LLM evaluator (Qwen3.5-plus) following the same guidelines covers the full set. In the experiments the Interpreter uses Qwen3-VL-Plus, the Coordinator, Adapter and Critic use Qwen3-Max, and the Execution Layer uses Qwen-Image-2.0.

Human evaluation

TransMeme has the best score on every dimension, a 33.1% average improvement over the strongest baseline. Its gains over all baselines are statistically significant on every dimension and on the average (two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, all p < 0.001, also after Bonferroni correction).

LLM-as-a-Judge on the full set

On all 1,000 memes the same ordering holds: TransMeme leads on every dimension and in both directions, and is ranked first on 60% of the memes. Validated on the 200 human-annotated memes, the evaluator's scores correlate with mean human ratings at Spearman 0.693 to 0.802 across the four dimensions, and its Top-1 choice agrees with the aggregated human choice in 89.0% of cases; agreement on full rankings is more moderate (mean Kendall's τ of 0.422). The paper uses it as a scalable proxy and keeps human evaluation as the primary reference.

Qualitative comparison

The paper shows a randomly selected set of examples. In the first column TransMeme replaces the character Loopy with Pikachu as a figure the target audience recognizes more readily, and in the fourth it replaces a red envelope with a transfer record. The baselines often produce literal or awkward target text, insufficient cultural adaptation, or a mismatch between image and text.

Component analysis and scope

Each component is analysed separately, with LLM evaluators. In text-level scoring on 200 instances, the DPO-trained Rewriter averages 4.177 over naturalness, cultural quality and humor, against 2.838 without DPO and 3.835 when Qwen3-Max replaces the rewriter. The Critic loop is entered by 125 of the 1,000 samples (106 Chinese-to-English, 19 English-to-Chinese), mostly for cultural mismatch (75) and intent drift (47). Cultural planning is triggered for 181 samples, 155 of them Chinese-to-English (Section 4.3 and Fig. 4 of the paper).

The paper also states what remains open. TransMeme is not ranked first on 400 of the 1,000 samples; a manual analysis attributes 67.8% of these to weak humor reconstruction, 27.0% to image-text mismatch and 5.3% to cultural knowledge gaps. The experiments cover Chinese–English transcreation and use models from a single family; the authors present this as a controlled test of the framework design and leave broader cross-family validation to future work.

The code repository contains the multi-agent pipeline, scripts for the compared systems, the annotation interface and the 1,000 final TransMeme outputs of the evaluation.
